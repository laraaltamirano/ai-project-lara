AI today

What we now first perceive as Artificial Intelligence is Generative AI, which is the typical Large Language Model machine that can hold human-like conversations and answer our questions and follow tasks. Examples of this are: ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, Meta AI, and others.

However, Artificial Intelligence is more about a broad field of computer science that focuses on building systems that can mimic human intelligence and recognize patterns with which it can make predictions. Simply put, AI aims to be capable of performing human tasks that in the past required human intelligence only.

Definition of AI

There is no universal definition of AI. Therefore, one description that can work is one developed by the AI Group of Experts at the OECD (AIGO) in 2018:

“An AI system consists of three main elements: (1) sensors to collect raw data inputs from the environment; (2) operational logic to interpret the data for a given set of objectives and provide an output of recommendations, predictions or decisions; and (3) actuators to change the environment based on that output. While sensors and actuators can be either machines or people, the key power of an AI system resides in its operational logic.”

Main benefits and best usages:

The European Council posted the main 5 benefits and main 5 risks, followed by its AI Act, which the EU released in order to regulate the potential risks of Artificial Intelligence.

Note: This page is very optimistic; people online tend to be more conservative or sceptic towards AI.

Benefits

  1. Improved process efficiency: automates tasks and optimises workflows, boosting productivity.
  2. Greater creativity: frees people from repetitive work for more creative tasks.
  3. Lower risk of error: faster, more precise work, e.g. in surgery or failure prediction.
  4. Performance of dangerous tasks: rescue, bomb disposal, hazardous substances.
  5. Resolution of global challenges: healthcare, climate change, food security.

Risks

  1. Opacity: unclear how AI decides; the law requires transparency to users.
  2. Lack of accountability: risk of misuse without clear guidelines.
  3. Risks to safety: errors in critical sectors; the law requires risk assessments and human oversight.
  4. Bias and discrimination: biased training data; the act includes measures to mitigate it.
  5. Violation of fundamental rights: threats to privacy and free expression; mass surveillance and social scoring are banned.
